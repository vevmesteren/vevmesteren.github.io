Three weeks after JPMorgan moved to block AI agents from customer accounts, the build side answered. OnePay launched the first consumer-facing agentic banking product: an agent gets its own spending pocket with per-purchase limits, monthly caps, single-use credentials that expire after each transaction, and one-toggle revocation. The scoped-pocket pattern is the production template — authorization that is time-limited, revocable, and logged.

Lead Bank published the matching policy framework, proposing an "Authorized Financial Agent" category that covers identity, authorization, liability, and supervision, with a model "Know Your Agent" program and a phased roadmap from supervisory guidance to legislation. It is the agent equivalent of KYC, and it arrived the same week.

Meanwhile the block side hardened. JPMorgan warned of risks to consumers who rely on AI agents; reporting confirmed it and other US banks are locking agents out of accounts. Self Labs shipped an "Agent Vault" for exactly that world — agents that need credentials banks won't issue. TD moved the other direction internally, expanding agentic AI across mortgage operations. Crossmint launched an agent commerce toolkit, and a Hong Kong bank tested agent micro-payments over tokenized deposits.

What moved this week is the shape of the answer. The question was never whether the interface becomes an agent the bank doesn't control — it does. The question is who builds the trust infrastructure that makes that safe. This week five institutions built pieces in three layers: product (OnePay's scoped pocket), policy (Lead's Authorized Financial Agent), and infrastructure (audit trails, tokenized rails). They converge on one pattern: scoped, time-limited, revocable authority with logging.

The bank that builds all of it natively — scoped authorization, audit trail, liability allocation, and the data layer — on its own balance sheet with its own data captures the trust position. AI alone is not the edge. The edge is the trust infrastructure a fintech can't build because it lacks the charter, the balance sheet, and the data.
